\subsection{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:kvprune-protocol}

All experiments are run on the HM3D-v2 validation split with deterministic action selection
and visualization disabled, at seed 17. We use two test sets: a 100-episode manifest, which
is the full protocol, and a 25-episode seed-17 subset of it, used as a promotion gate to
decide which arms merit a full-scale run. We refer to the latter as the \emph{screen}.
Reported significance is a paired sign-flip bootstrap against the baseline of the same
batch.

Budgets are matched to measured recency-window memory, so that a budget arm and its
corresponding window arm hold the same number of resident KV slots. With a $\approx
574$-token pinned prompt prefix and $\approx 153$ tokens per frame surviving the sparse
visual filter, the window-equivalent budgets are $B_4 = 1187$, $B_8 = 1799$ and $B_{16} =
3022$ slots. One slot costs $\approx 0.109$\,MB in bf16 (28 layers $\times$ 8 KV heads
$\times$ 128 dimensions $\times$ 2). Unless stated otherwise, every arm uses a 32-frame
selection pool, and reports success rate (SR), success weighted by path length (SPL), mean
episode length in steps, mean resident KV slots, and mean per-step latency.

\subsection{Can any selector beat random?}
\label{sec:kvprune-selectors}

Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-selectors} compares the information-seeking selectors against random
at all three budgets. This batch was re-baselined, and its random arm differs from that of
Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-budget} ($B_4$: 0.760 against 0.730); comparisons are therefore valid
only within a batch. No selector separates from random at any budget. The \code{grid}
selector is the most controlled of these: it reuses random's own draw to fix the per-frame
quotas and replaces only the choice of patches \emph{within} a frame with a spatially even
one, so any difference would be attributable to within-frame coverage alone. There is none.

Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-screen} reports every selector screened at $B_4$ on the 25-episode
subset, which is where the selector search was actually conducted; only arms that survived
this gate were run at full scale. The ordering is stable and unambiguous. The \code{grid}
selector is the only arm indistinguishable from random. Leave-one-frame-out \code{kl} is
already slightly behind at eight times the latency. Every remaining selector is clearly
worse, and the ranking is informative about \emph{why}: the two arms that impose an explicit
coverage criterion --- equal-per-frame stratification and farthest-point diversity over the
sparse patch embeddings --- lose $0.16$ SR, and the gradient-based \code{fisher} score is the
worst arm in the series at $0.440$ SR, less than random at a $2\times$ smaller budget.

Geometry-based selection fails in the same direction. Tagging each visual slot with the world
voxel its patch observes, then capping or equalising slots per occupied cell, costs $0.07$ to
$0.10$ SPL, and coarser cells hurt more. The loss concentrates on the \emph{short} episodes,
those random solves quickly: capping per cell removes the repeated close-range views of
nearby floor and doorway geometry in the frames just outside the protected window, which is
the dense local detail short-range navigation depends on. Geometric redundancy is used, not
wasted.

Taken together, every selector that imposes structure on the retained set --- temporal
(stratified), appearance (diversity), geometric (voxel), or gradient-based (fisher) --- lands
below an i.i.d.\ draw, while the two that reproduce random's own allocation (\code{grid},
which copies its per-frame quotas, and \code{kl}, whose frame-proportional quotas approximate
them) tie with it.

\subsection{Protecting the action-text history}
\label{sec:kvprune-scope}

The arms above let visual patches and the agent's own action text compete for the same
budget. Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-scope} instead pins every in-window text slot and prunes only
visual slots, asking whether the action history --- the agent's odometry in words --- is
worth protecting. It is not. At $B_8$ this swaps roughly 290 visual slots for text and costs
$0.15$ SR, so per slot a visual token is worth considerably more than an old action token,
and the unrestricted scope was already making the better trade by letting text thin out.
The $B_4$ budget is structurally infeasible under this scope, because the protected floor
exceeds the budget once the window fills. The tie between \code{kl} and random replicates
here, in a second regime.

\subsection{Layer-selective pruning}
\label{sec:kvprune-layers}

Every arm above prunes all 28 decoder layers with one slot set. We next confine the budget
to layers $[L, 28)$, leaving the remaining layers with the window-only cache, so that $L=0$
recovers the uniform arms. Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-layer-screen} sweeps the prune layer on the
screen. The ordering is informative: \code{kl} is \emph{worse} than random when the drop
happens at shallow layers, and only turns positive at $L = 16$--$20$. Those two layers were
promoted.

Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-layer-full} reports the promoted layers at full scale. Paired
\code{kl} $-$ random gives $\Delta$SR $+0.03$ ($p = 0.46$) and $\Delta$SPL $+0.018$
($p = 0.27$) at $L = 16$, and $\Delta$SR $+0.01$ ($p = 1.0$) and $\Delta$SPL $+0.016$
($p = 0.12$) at $L = 20$. The direction agrees with the screen on both metrics at both
layers, making this the first selector to sit above random on SPL anywhere in the series,
but the effect is not detectable at $n = 100$ --- an SPL gap of this size would require on
the order of 1000 paired episodes --- and it costs roughly nine times the per-step latency.
Two further readings follow. First, where one prunes matters only through memory: at matched
layer-mean KV the layer arms sit \emph{on} the uniform-random budget curve, so sparing the
shallow layers buys exactly what the extra slots buy and no more. Second, \code{kl}'s failure
at shallow layers is diagnostic: leave-one-frame-out KL measured at the pruned layers keeps
what is salient to the current decision, which is redundant with the protected recent turns,
while at layers $\ge 16$ so little history influence remains to rank that the selector
degenerates towards uniform sampling and matches random.

\subsection{The visual information horizon}
\label{sec:kvprune-blind}

Table~\ref{tab:kvprune-blind} reports a hypothesis test rather than a compression method. On
decoder layers $\ge L$, no query may attend to any visual slot --- cached history and the
current frame alike --- and those slots leave the blind layers' caches. Text and the 32-frame
pool are untouched, and no budget or selector is involved; $L = 28$ is the unmodified model
and $L = 0$ is a text-only policy.

The horizon is real and sharp. Blinding from layer 24 is indistinguishable from the full
model. Blinding from layer 20 leaves the agent finding the goal as often, but taking
$\approx 9\%$ more steps, a significant SPL loss of $0.05$: layers 20--27 still read some
visual detail, and what they read buys path efficiency rather than success. Blinding from
layer 16 collapses the policy, and blinding from layer 0 fails entirely. The visual
information that decides the action is therefore consumed almost entirely within layers
16--20, which matches both the leave-one-frame-out horizon curve and the layer-selective
result above. Two consequences are practical: layers $\ge 24$ can drop every visual slot at
no cost, and any future compression targeting layers below 20 must preserve what those layers
absorb.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:kvprune-summary}

Across every batch, no selector beats random pruning by a measurable margin at any budget,
any candidate scope, or any prune layer. The two arms that ever edge above it --- \code{kl}
at $L = 16$ and $L = 20$ --- do so within noise and at roughly nine times the per-step
latency. Random's geometric attrition of dense recent frames appears to be the operating
point of this policy, and the remaining lever is training under the budget rather than
selection.
